Abstract

Aesthetic image cropping (AIC) aims to improve image composition and aesthetic quality by removing extraneous or distracting content. Over the past decade, AIC methods have evolved from specialized vision models to multimodal large language models (MLLMs). Reliable evaluation that reflects human preferences is essential for benchmarking AIC methods and guiding model optimization. However, the current AIC evaluation relies largely on geometry-based metrics such as intersection over union (IoU), as well as image aesthetic assessment(IAA) models. Although inconsistencies with human preferences have long been observed, the robustness and reliability of these evaluation criteria remain underexplored. To bridge this gap, we first construct CropBench, a large-scale benchmark comprising 106,995 human preference pairs for 1,019 images, with each candidate set containing the original image and candidate crops produced by 14 AIC methods. A systematic study on CropBench reveals that the existing criteria, ranging from IoU to off-the-shelf IAA models, only exhibit limited alignment with human preferences. Building upon CropBench, we further propose CropReward, an MLLM-based reward model that evaluates a candidate crop by comparing with the original uncropped image and outputs a score indicating explicit aesthetic improvement or degradation relative to the original. In addition, we employ CropReward to construct CropReward-300K, the largest AIC dataset to date, and further develop CropAlign, a preference-aligned AIC model trained solely with CropReward supervision, without human-annotated crop boxes. Experimental results demonstrate that CropReward aligns with human preferences significantly better than existing AIC evaluation criteria. CropAlign also surpasses the state-of-the-art AIC methods, demonstrating the advantage of CropReward in guiding AIC model optimization. Models and datasets will be made publicly available.

I Introduction

Aesthetic image cropping (AIC) aims to improve the composition and aesthetic quality of an image by removing extraneous or distracting content [1, 2]. AIC has widespread applications in photography, content creation and editing, and thumbnail generation [3, 1]. Over the past decade, AIC has made substantial progress through deep learning, with methods evolving from specialized vision models [2, 4] to multimodal large language models (MLLMs) [5, 6, 7]. For the same image, different methods may produce multiple plausible crops with subtle differences in content and composition. Reliable evaluation that reflects human preferences over cropping results is therefore essential for benchmarking AIC methods and guiding model optimization.

In the literature, AIC evaluation has relied heavily on geometry-based metrics [4, 5, 8], among which intersection over union (IoU) is the most representative, althouth its inconsistency with human preferences has long been observed [2]. As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), metrics like IoU require expert-annotated reference boxes and measure the spatial alignment between a predicted crop and these references. An image may admit multiple pleasing crops with different compositions, so deviating from the reference box does not necessarily indicate a bad crop. Moreover, these metrics only consider box coordinates and may assign similar scores to a crop that preserves the main subject and another one that cuts off part of it. Recent work has also explored using image aesthetic assessment (IAA) models to evaluate cropping results directly [5, 9]. However, scoring a crop in isolation does not directly account for what has been removed or how the composition has changed relative to the original image. A high aesthetic score therefore does not necessarily mean that people prefer the crop to the original. In the example at the bottom of Fig. 1, VILA [10] ranks crop C first, although annotators prefer the original image to this crop. These limitations motivate us to rethink existing evaluation criteria and raise a central question:

How should we comprehensively and fairly measure the performance of AIC models?

We argue that reliable evaluation of AIC methods should meet two requirements. (1) Assessing the effectiveness of cropping. Cropping may improve or degrade the aesthetic quality of the original image. Evaluation should therefore assess whether people prefer the resulting crop to the original image or vice versa. (2) Distinguishing subtle differences among candidate crops. Crops of the same image may differ slightly in their boundaries, yet these differences can affect the content and composition of the resulting crops. Evaluation model should faithfully reflect human preferences among similar crops. However, how well existing evaluation criteria meet the above requirements remains unclear.

To address the above challenges, we first construct CropBench, a large-scale human preference benchmark for AIC task. It comprises 106,995 human-annotated preference pairs of crops produced by 14 AIC methods on 1,019 images covering both general and portrait photography. The original image is also included when forming the preference pairs, and all candidate pairs are annotated with an explicit option for ties. We further calculate preference scores using a tie-aware Bradley–Terry (BT) model, with the score of the original image anchored at zero within each candidate set. Based on CropBench, we conduct a systematic study of existing AIC evaluation criteria, ranging from IoU to state-of-the-art IAA models, revealing that all existing criteria achieve only limited consistency with human preferences. CropBench also supports benchmarking of current AIC methods. The results reveal a phenomenon that is often overlooked in AIC: even the top-ranked models sometimes produce crops that people prefer less than the original image.

Building on the above findings, we further propose CropReward, an MLLM-based reward model trained on the preference annotations of CropBench, which satisfies the two aforementioned requirements. As illustrated in Fig. 1(b), CropReward takes both the original image and a candidate crop as input, allowing it to consider what is retained and what is removed without requiring expert-annotated reference boxes. Its output is expressed relative to the original image, with positive and negative scores indicating predicted improvement and degradation in aesthetic quality, respectively. To distinguish similar crops, we further introduce a pairwise difference learning objective, based on which the model is trained to predict the difference in human preference scores between two crops. We further explore the potential of CropReward for guiding AIC model optimization. We first use it to select crops generated by existing AIC methods on a large number of unlabeled images, based on which we build CropReward-300K, a large-scale AIC dataset containing over 300,000 original images, each paired with a CropReward-selected crop box. We then develop CropAlign, an MLLM-based cropping model trained through supervised fine-tuning on this dataset and further optimized through reinforcement learning with CropReward as the reward.

The contributions of this work are threefold:

  • •

    We build CropBench, a large-scale human preference benchmark for AIC task, using a hybrid annotation strategy that determines the number of human annotators per pair based on agreement among five MLLM judges. The benchmark contains 106,995 pairwise labels covering outputs from 14 AIC models and the corresponding original images, supporting systematic evaluation of both existing criteria and AIC methods.

  • •

    We propose CropReward, an MLLM-based reward model that learns fine-grained cropping preferences to assess whether cropping improves or degrades the original composition, which aligns substantially better with human preferences than existing criteria.

  • •

    We construct CropReward-300K, a large-scale AIC dataset curated by CropReward without the need of human annotation, and develop CropAlign, an advanced cropping model optimized with CropReward that achieves the state-of-the-art performance on multiple benchmarks, including CropBench, and existing cropping datasets, FLMS and FCDB.

II Related Work

II-A Aesthetic Image Cropping

Early AIC methods searched for crop windows using handcrafted cues such as saliency, composition rules, and boundary simplicity [11]. Two common paradigms in deep learning-based AIC are crop regression and candidate ranking, as illustrated in Fig. 2(a) and Fig. 2(b). Crop regression methods directly predict a crop box from the input image [4, 16, 17]. The composition-aware cropping network (CACNet) [4] recognizes composition patterns to guide crop prediction. To alleviate reliance on expert annotations, Hong et al. [16] outpainted professional photographs, treating the original image as the target crop of a larger outpainted image. By contrast, candidate ranking methods score a set of candidate crops and select the highest-scoring one [19, 13, 14, 20, 2, 21, 22, 15]. Wei et al. [13] trained a crop scoring network using human judgments of relative crop quality. Zeng et al. [14, 2] proposed grid anchor-based image cropping (GAIC), which uses grid anchors to generate candidate crops and a lightweight model to score them. In crop regression, training against a target box penalizes deviations from that box, even when alternative crops are equally good. Candidate ranking can learn human preferences among crops. However, when the original image is excluded from the training comparisons, these preferences do not establish whether a crop is preferred to the original.

Beyond direct regression and ranking, Li et al. [23] introduced aesthetics-aware reinforcement learning (A2-RL), an early attempt to optimize cropping through a sequence of window-adjustment actions. Its reward was derived from an image aesthetic classifier rather than learned from human preferences over crop decisions. More recently, MLLMs have been applied to AIC [5, 6, 7]. Sheng et al. [6] applied instruction tuning to MLLMs for AIC. Du et al. [7] first trained an MLLM to identify aesthetic problems and suggest improvements, then fine-tuned it using chain-of-thought rationales to predict crop boxes and explain the cropping decisions. In this work, we train CropReward on human preferences among candidate crops with the original image included. Then the score of CropReward naturally serves as a better reward for optimizing a cropping model without human-annotated crop boxes, as illustrated in Fig. 2(c).

II-B AIC Datasets and Evaluation Protocols

Table I summarizes representative AIC datasets categorized by their candidate sources and annotation types. Expert-crop datasets [3, 11, 12] provide one or several human-annotated crop boxes for each image. These boxes serve as training targets and as references for geometry-based evaluation. Candidate-scoring datasets [13, 14, 2, 15] collect human judgments of crop quality over candidates generated by existing algorithms, grid anchors, or random sampling. These annotations support training and evaluation of candidate ranking models. Synthetic-crop datasets [16, 17] construct training pairs automatically through outpainting and pseudo-crop refinement. FGAesthetics [18] collects pairwise preferences among visually similar images for fine-grained aesthetic assessment. Its cropping subset contains 1,702 series derived from CPC and GAICD datasets, comprising 10,554 crops and 25,644 preference pairs.

Evaluation protocols depend on the types of annotations. IoU and boundary displacement error (Disp) measure the geometric alignment between predicted and reference crop boxes, whereas ranking metrics such as AccK/N\mathrm{Acc}_{K/N} and SRCC compare the predicted scores of candidate crops with their mean opinion scores (MOS) [2]. However, these protocols do not accurately assess whether people prefer the predicted crops relative to the original image or vice versa. CropBench comprises the crops of diverse AIC models and the original image in each candidate set, with human preference annotations for all candidate pairs and an explicit option for ties. Expert-annotated crop boxes are also provided for computing geometric metrics. These annotations support various AIC evaluation tasks, including benchmarking AIC methods, evaluating how well existing criteria reflect human preferences, and training a reward model.

II-C Image Aesthetic and Composition Assessment

IAA predicts the aesthetic quality of an image, typically as a score or a distribution of human ratings [24]. To preserve composition and fine details during feature extraction, some methods adopt multi-scale image representations [25, 26]. Beyond visual features alone, vision-language-based methods learn aesthetic representations from images paired with aesthetic comments [27, 10], and MLLM-based models go a step further by producing natural-language explanations of aesthetic judgments [28, 29]. More recently, learning from relative rankings has been explored to distinguish aesthetic differences among similar images [18].

Image composition assessment (ICA) focuses on the spatial arrangement of visual elements. Composition scoring methods model salient regions and their arrangement under different composition patterns [30]. Recent MLLM-based work further explores explanations of composition quality and guidance for improving composition [31, 32].

IAA and ICA models have also been used to evaluate cropping results [5, 9]. However, the discriminative ability of the existing IAA/ICA models are typically limited. In addition, an absolute aesthetic or composition score does not directly indicate whether a crop is preferred to the original image. In this work, we propose CropReward to learn this kind of comparison from human cropping preferences, using both the original image and the crop as input.

II-D Reward Models for Visual Preference Alignment

Reward models learn to predict which outputs people prefer and can be used to evaluate generative models and optimize them through reinforcement learning. They have been studied in text-to-image generation [33, 34, 35, 36], with subsequent applications to image editing [37, 38], photo retouching [39], and image restoration [40]. In this work, CropReward is trained on human preferences among candidate crops and between crops and their original uncropped images. It evaluates each crop together with the original image and provides a positive/negative score relative to the original, which can serve as both an evaluation criterion and a reward for AIC model optimization.

III CropBench: A Human-Preference Benchmark for AIC

CropBench provides human preference annotations for benchmarking AIC methods, which is the foundation for assessing existing evaluation criteria and training a reward model. It consists of candidate crop sets and each candidate set contains both the outputs of AIC models and the original uncropped image. We annotate all candidate pairs with an explicit option for ties, allowing comparisons both among crops and with the original image. Fig. 4 illustrates the four stages of dataset construction, and Fig. 4 shows examples of candidate crop sets from different categories, along with the number of sets in each category.

III-A Image Collection

CropBench covers images from both general scenes and portrait photography. For general scenes, eight common categories are considered, including buildings, animals, food, indoor scenes, scenery, night scenes, still life, and plants. For portrait photography, single, group, and environmental portraits are included. Images are drawn from the PARA dataset [41], supplemented by 160 self-collected portrait photographs. We focus on images whose composition has potential for improvement through cropping. An MLLM is first filters out that are already well-composed, would benefit only marginally, or would be harmed by cropping. Three aesthetic experts then review the remaining images. This screening removes 50.4% of the initial general pool and 55.0% of the portrait pool.

We sample the remaining images across aesthetic levels, image orientations, and scene/subject categories. Three aesthetic levels are defined using the ratings provided by PARA for general images and HumanAesExpert [28] scores for portraits. The final benchmark contains 510 general images and 509 portrait images, with median shorter-side lengths of 1,475 and 1,627 pixels, respectively.

III-B Candidate Crop Generation

The candidate crop pool is built from the outputs of real cropping models that span the paradigm spectrum of Sec. II. Specifically, we deploy 10 specialized AIC models, including the regression-based CACNet [4], the ranking-based VFN [19], VEN [13], GAIC [2], HCIC [22], CGS [21], and S2CNet [15], the RL-based A2-RL [23], and the MLLM-based InstructCrop [6] and Venus [7], together with 4 general-purpose MLLMs, namely GPT-5.5 [42], Qwen3.5-4B [43], Qwen3.6-27B [44], and Qwen3.7-Plus [45]. Each model contributes one crop per image. All specialized AIC models are applied with their officially released weights. For candidate ranking methods, we select the top-ranked crop; for other methods, we use the crop they produce directly. The four general-purpose MLLMs use the same prompt, which requests a single crop box in normalized coordinates. Including the original uncropped image produces 15 candidates per image and 15,285 candidates in total. Comparing all (152)=105\binom{15}{2}=105 pairs per image yields 106,995 pairs for annotation.

III-C Hybrid Preference Annotation

Collecting human preferences for 106,995 candidate pairs requires substantial annotation effort. To address this challenge, we design a human-machine collaborative annotation (HMCA) paradigm. Specifically, we use MLLM votes to determine how many human annotators assess each pair, while humans provide all final labels. For each comparison, the judge is shown the original image followed by two candidates and asked to choose candidate A, candidate B, or equivalent (E). Five MLLMs first judge every pair independently, including Gemini 3.5 Flash [46], Claude Opus 4.8 [47], Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro [48], Kimi K2.5 [49], and MiMo-V2.5 [50]. These judges are different from the candidate-generating models in Sec. III-B. Agreement among the five MLLM votes determines the number of human annotators assigned to each pair. Pairs with unanimous votes (5:0:0) are assigned to two annotators, pairs with vote counts of 4:1:0 or 3:1:1 to four annotators, and pairs with vote counts of 3:2:0 or 2:2:1 to six annotators. Human annotators do not see the MLLM votes. If the assigned human annotators disagree, an additional annotator reviews the pair in a second round. Final labels are determined by majority voting. All 106,995 pairs are annotated by a pool of twenty graduate students specializing in image aesthetics and composition assessment.

We additionally collect three expert-annotated crop boxes per image for geometric evaluation. Three annotators independently select one crop box for each image, aiming to improve overall composition while preserving important visual content and removing distracting regions. The annotation is performed directly on the original images, without referring to model-generated candidates. These reference boxes are separate from the 15 candidates used for preference annotation.

III-D BT-score Calculation

We calculate a preference score for each candidate crop from the pairwise annotations. where Zi​j=eui+euj+2​ν​e(ui+uj)/2Z_{ij}=e^{u_{i}}+e^{u_{j}}+2\nu\,e^{(u_{i}+u_{j})/2} and ν≥0\nu\geq 0 controls the probability of a tie, and ≡\equiv denotes an equivalent preference between two candidate crops. where yi​jy_{ij} is the annotated label. The Gaussian prior with σ=3\sigma=3 limits extreme scores for candidate crops that consistently win or lose. We anchor the score of the original image at zero, so that positive and negative scores indicate improvement and degradation relative to the original image, respectively. CropBench is split by image into a training set of 819 images (85,995 pairs) and a test set of 200 images (21,000 pairs). All candidate crops and comparisons from an image belong to the same split.

III-E Benchmark Analysis

We compare the 14 AIC models using their BT scores. Fig. 5(a) shows the score distributions for general and portrait images. To examine the relationship between cropping extent and human preferences, Fig. 5(b) shows the mean retained-area ratio for each AIC model. We have three observations.

Observation 1: A general-purpose MLLM outperforms specialized AIC models. GPT-5.5 achieves the highest mean BT score for both image categories under zero-shot evaluation, exceeding all ten evaluated specialized AIC models. Venus achieves the second-highest mean score. Significant performance variations have also been observed across MLLMs. These results motivate us to study whether human preference supervision can further improve AIC models.

Observation 2: Cropping does not always improve the composition of the original image. Several methods have negative mean BT scores. Even the highest-ranked methods may produce some crops with negative scores. These results highlight the need to assess a crop relative to its original, even when the method performs well on average.

Observation 3: Methods differ substantially in cropping extent. A2-RL retains an average of 86.9% of the original image area for general images and 86.0% for portraits, whereas Qwen3.5-4B retains only 25.4% and 23.6%, respectively. GPT-5.5 achieves higher mean BT scores than the above two methods while retaining approximately 72% of the image area for each image category. Retained area alone is thus not informative about crop quality, which also depends on what is kept and how it is composed.

IV CropReward: A Human-Aligned Reward Model for AIC

IV-A Formulation

CropReward learns a scoring function sθ​(I,c)s_{\theta}(I,c) that takes an original image II and a candidate crop cc as input and returns a scalar score. For each training image, let 𝒞I={c0,c1,…,c14}\mathcal{C}_{I}=\{c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{14}\} denote the corresponding candidate crop set, where c0=Ic_{0}=I is the original image. Each candidate cic_{i} has a BT preference score uiu_{i} estimated from human annotations, with u0=0u_{0}=0 as defined in Sec. III-D. We train the model to match differences in these preference scores:

sθ​(I,ci)−sθ​(I,cj)≈ui−uj,0≤i<j≤14.s_{\theta}(I,c_{i})-s_{\theta}(I,c_{j})\;\approx\;u_{i}-u_{j},\qquad 0\leq i<j\leq 14. (3)

This objective includes comparisons among candidate crops and between each crop and the original image. We implement it using the normalized pairwise difference (NPD) loss in Sec. IV-C. Expert-annotated reference crop boxes are not required for training or inference.

IV-B Model Architecture

We build CropReward on Qwen3.5-4B [43]. As illustrated in Fig. 6(a), each candidate crop is scored independently by comparing with the original image with an evaluation prompt. The prompt asks the model to assess composition improvement and content preservation. We append a learnable <AES> token to the prompt and use its final-layer hidden state, denoted by 𝐡i\mathbf{h}_{i}, to predict the candidate score through a two-layer MLP head ℛω\mathcal{R}_{\omega}:

si=sθ​(I,ci)=ℛω​(𝐡i).s_{i}=s_{\theta}(I,c_{i})=\mathcal{R}_{\omega}(\mathbf{h}_{i}). (4)

We train the visual patch merger, low-rank adaptation (LoRA) adapters with rank 16 in the language model, the <AES> embedding, and the MLP head. The remaining pretrained parameters are frozen, giving 62M trainable parameters in total.

IV-C Pairwise Difference Learning

To implement the learning objective in Eq. (3), we use the NPD loss to compare predicted and BT score differences over all candidate pairs, as illustrated in Fig. 6(b):

ℒ=S|𝒫|​∑(i,j)∈𝒫ℓδ​(si−sjS,ui−ujS),\mathcal{L}=\frac{S}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}}\ell_{\delta}\!\left(\frac{s_{i}-s_{j}}{S},\;\frac{u_{i}-u_{j}}{S}\right), (5)

where 𝒫\mathcal{P} contains all (152)=105\binom{15}{2}=105 unordered pairs, ℓδ\ell_{\delta} is the smooth-ℓ1\ell_{1} loss with transition parameter δ\delta, and S>0S>0 is a scale estimated from the training set and fixed throughout training. The transition point δ\delta is defined in normalized units.

Unlike a ranking loss that uses only preference order, NPD also supervises the difference in BT preference scores. All candidate pairs contribute to the loss, including those annotated as equivalent. We train CropReward jointly on general and portrait images from the CropBench training split. Optimization settings are provided in Sec. VI-A.

IV-D Inference

The NPD loss supervises differences between candidate scores. At inference, we score the original image as a candidate, obtaining sθ​(I,I)s_{\theta}(I,I). The final score of a crop is computed relative to the original image:

rθ​(I,c)=sθ​(I,c)−sθ​(I,I),r_{\theta}(I,c)=s_{\theta}(I,c)-s_{\theta}(I,I), (6)

where cc is a candidate crop of the original image II.

A positive score predicts a preference for the crop, while a negative score predicts a preference for the original image. Subtracting the original image score preserves the ranking of candidates from the same image.

V Optimizing AIC Models with CropReward

In this section, we use CropReward to select training crops from a large number of unlabeled images and to optimize a cropping model through reinforcement learning.

V-A Construction of CropReward-300K Dataset

To obtain training data without additional human annotation, we use CropReward to select crops generated by existing AIC models on unlabeled images. The selected crops serve as labels for CropReward-300K. We start with 517,889 Unsplash images, comprising 249,797 general images and 268,092 portrait images. For each of the general and portrait domains, we selected the eight top-ranked AIC models on CropBench to generate eight candidate crops per image. We evaluate these eight crops using the relative score rθ​(I,c)r_{\theta}(I,c) defined in Eq. (6) and select the highest-scoring crop as the pseudo label. We then rank images by the scores of their selected crops and retain the top 60% within each domain. The resulting dataset contains 310,735 images, including 149,879 general images and 160,856 portraits. Each image is paired with the selected crop, its CropReward score, and the method that produced it. Fig. 7 shows the mean CropReward scores of the eight models in each domain.

V-B Reward-Guided Cropping

Based on CropReward-300K, we first apply supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to Qwen3.5-4B to predict a single crop box in normalized coordinates, producing CropAlign-SFT. We then train CropAlign by optimizing CropAlign-SFT with group relative policy optimization (GRPO) [53], using CropReward to evaluate the crops it generates, as illustrated in Fig. 8. During this stage, the prompt asks for a textual rationale followed by a crop box, and CropReward remains frozen.

Given an original image II and a task prompt qq, the policy πϕ\pi_{\phi} samples GG responses {o(1),…,o(G)}\{o^{(1)},\ldots,o^{(G)}\}. A response is valid if it follows the required output format and contains coordinates defining a valid crop of II. For each valid response, we extract the box b(i)b^{(i)} and apply it to II to obtain the crop c(i)c^{(i)}. We use the relative score from CropReward to construct the reward:

R(i)={σ⁡(rθ​(I,c(i)))+λf,if ​o(i)​ is valid,0,otherwise,R^{(i)}=\begin{cases}\sigma\!\left(r_{\theta}(I,c^{(i)})\right)+\lambda_{f},&\text{if }o^{(i)}\text{ is valid},\\[2.0pt] 0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases} (7)

where σ⁡(⋅)\sigma(\cdot) is the sigmoid function, which maps the relative crop score to (0,1)(0,1), and λf\lambda_{f} is a format bonus. Rewards are normalized within each group to compute relative advantages. GRPO then updates the policy using a clipped policy-gradient objective with a KL penalty relative to a frozen reference policy. The RL stage optimizes the policy with these rewards without target crop boxes, as illustrated in Fig. 2(c). Neither stage requires additional human annotation.

VI Experiments

In this section, we conduct extensive experiments and comparisons on CropBench and popular AIC datasets to validate the performance of the proposed model from the following four aspects.

  • •

    Alignment with Human Preferences: We evaluate how well existing AIC evaluation criteria agree with human cropping preferences and whether CropReward achieves better alignment (see Sec. VI-B).

  • •

    Generalization: We examine whether CropReward generalizes to datasets beyond CropBench (see Sec. VI-C).

  • •

    Ablation Studies: We examine the effects of the training objective and the inclusion of the original image as input on the performance of CropReward (see Sec. VI-D).

  • •

    Effectiveness of Reward: We assess the effectiveness of CropReward in guiding AIC model optimization by comparing CropAlign with state-of-the-art AIC models (see Sec. VI-E).

VI-A Experimental Setup

Datasets. We use five datasets for evaluation.

  • •

    CropBench is used to evaluate agreement with human cropping preferences. It contains 819 training images and 200 test images (100 per domain), with 85,995 and 21,000 annotated pairs, respectively. We report agreement results on the test split.

  • •

    GAICD-v2 [2] and FGAesthetics-Crop [18] are used to evaluate model generalization. We use the 500-image test split of GAICD-v2. FGAesthetics-Crop is the cropping subset of FGAesthetics, and we use the official test split, which contains 168 image series, 990 candidate crops, and 2,088 preference pairs.

  • •

    FLMS [11] and FCDB [12], together with the test set of CropBench, are used to evaluate cropping models. We use all 500 FLMS images, with up to ten expert crops per image, and the 345 valid FCDB test images, each with a single expert crop.

Evaluation protocols. On CropBench, we compute metrics for each set of 15 candidates with the original image included, and average the results over images within each domain. We measure agreement between predicted scores and BT preference scores using the Pearson linear correlation coefficient (PLCC) and Spearman rank correlation coefficient (SRCC). ACCpair\mathrm{ACC}_{\mathrm{pair}} measures the accuracy of predicting a preference for A, a preference for B, or equivalence (E) over the 105 annotated pairs of each image. ACCtop1\mathrm{ACC}_{\mathrm{top1}} and ACCtop3\mathrm{ACC}_{\mathrm{top3}} measure how often the candidate with the highest predicted score is among the top one or top three candidates ranked by their BT preference scores, respectively. On FGAesthetics-Crop, ss-ACC measures the accuracy of selecting the most preferred crop in each series, while ss-SRCC measures the rank correlation within each series.

Implementation details. We train CropReward for three epochs using AdamW, with a learning rate of 2.5×10−52.5{\times}10^{-5} and a weight decay of 0.01. The learning rate is linearly warmed up over the first 3% of training steps and then decayed to zero with a cosine schedule. The original image and candidate crop are resized independently while preserving their aspect ratios, with each image containing at most 512×512512{\times}512 pixels. LoRA adapters with rank 16, scaling factor 16, and dropout 0.05 are applied to the attention and feed-forward projection layers of the language model. The scale SS in the NPD loss (Eq. (5)) is estimated from the training split and fixed at 10.38. For CropAlign, we first fine-tune Qwen3.5-4B on the full CropReward-300K for two epochs using LoRA with rank 16. GRPO training then runs for one epoch with a group size of G=4G=4, a KL coefficient of 0.01, a format bonus of λf=0.05\lambda_{f}=0.05, and a sampling temperature of 1.0. All training runs use two NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPUs.

VI-B Alignment with Human Preferences

Table II reports agreement with human cropping preferences on CropBench. The comparison includes geometry-based metrics, off-the-shelf models for IAA, ICA and human preference modeling, three existing evaluators fine-tuned on CropBench, and CropReward. We have the following observations.

Expert crop boxes remain valuable references for AIC evaluation. IoUmean outperforms all evaluated off-the-shelf models on every metric for both image categories, demonstrating the value of high-quality expert crop boxes for evaluating cropping results. Among the three IoU variants, it also gives the best or joint-best results. Averaging IoU over all three expert crop boxes reduces dependence on any single reference. Nevertheless, agreement with reference boxes does not fully reflect human preferences. An image can have multiple well-composed crops, and a crop that differs from the reference boxes may still be preferred to one with higher IoU. These IoU scores also require expert-annotated reference boxes, limiting their use as rewards on unlabeled images.

Off-the-shelf models perform worse than geometry- based metric. FGAesQ [18] achieves the highest PLCC, SRCC, and pairwise accuracy among the IAA models. Its training on relative rankings of visually similar images, including crops, may help it distinguish subtle aesthetic differences between candidates. The GAIC evaluator [2] performs best on these three metrics among all off-the-shelf composition and crop scoring models. It was trained on mean opinion scores for predefined crop candidates. This result suggests that crop-specific supervision is useful for learning human cropping preferences. Among the reward models, EditReward [37] performs best on most metrics. Unlike ImageReward and HPSv3, EditReward takes both the original image and the edited result as input, which may help explain its stronger agreement with human cropping preferences than the other two reward models. Across all off-the-shelf models, however, top-1 accuracy reaches at most 0.150 on general images and 0.130 on portraits, showing that they rarely select the candidate most preferred by annotators.

CropReward aligns most closely with human preferences. To compare models trained on the same preference annotations, we also fine-tune the GAIC evaluator, Charm, and FGAesQ on the CropBench training split following their official implementations. As shown in Table II, fine-tuning improves all three models, but CropReward still outperforms all evaluated criteria on every metric for both image categories. Without using any expert-annotated crop boxes as references, CropReward achieves state-of-the-art performance, with SRCC of 0.763 and 0.787 on general and portrait images, respectively, exceeding IoUmean by 0.140 (22.47%) and 0.130 (19.79%). It also exceeds the strongest fine-tuned baseline, the GAIC evaluator, by 0.135 (21.50%) and 0.170 (27.55%) in SRCC, respectively. Pairwise accuracy reaches 0.761 and 0.777, while top-3 accuracy reaches 0.600 and 0.590. We examine the effects of the model input and training objective through ablation studies in Sec. VI-D.

Qualitative analysis. Fig. 9 compares existing AIC evaluation criteria with CropReward on four test examples. Existing criteria can disagree with human preferences both when comparing crops with the original image and when ranking alternative crops. In the first general scene example, FGAesQ ranks the original image above crops A, B, and C, although annotators prefer all three crops to the original. Selecting its highest-scoring candidate would retain the original image rather than a crop preferred by annotators. In the second general scene example, Charm assigns its highest score to crop D, which annotators rank below the original image and all other displayed crops. In contrast, CropReward aligns more closely with human preferences than the compared evaluation criteria in these examples. In the first portrait scene example, CropReward assigns negative scores to all four crops, correctly reflecting the annotators’ preference for the original image. Even with three expert reference boxes, IoUmean can misrank visually similar candidates: in the second portrait scene example, it places crop B above A, contrary to human preferences. Higher overlap with expert boxes therefore does not necessarily indicate a more preferred crop.

VI-C Generalization to External Benchmarks

We evaluate CropReward on GAICD-v2 and FGAesthetics-Crop to examine its generalization beyond CropBench. The two benchmarks differ from CropBench in their candidate crops and annotation types. GAICD-v2 provides MOS for grid-anchor candidates, whereas FGAesthetics-Crop provides pairwise preferences among visually similar crops derived from GAICD and CPC [13]. We use CropReward trained on CropBench without further fine-tuning. Table III compares its performance with representative existing models, including models trained on the corresponding benchmark. On GAICD-v2, CropReward achieves PLCC of 0.766 and SRCC of 0.716, outperforming all compared out-of-domain models. The strongest of these baselines, ArtiMuse, achieves 0.413 and 0.386, respectively. On FGAesthetics-Crop, CropReward achieves pair-level ACC and F1 of 0.822 and series-level ss-ACC and ss-SRCC of 0.526 and 0.662. It even surpasses the in-domain FGAesQ on all four metrics without fine-tuning on FGAesthetics. These results indicate that CropReward, trained on CropBench, aligns with both human quality ratings and fine-grained crop preferences on the two external benchmarks.

VI-D Ablation Studies

We examine two design choices of CropReward on the CropBench test split.

Training objective. Table V compares the NPD loss in Eq. (5) with pointwise score regression, ℒscore\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{score}}, and the pairwise logistic ranking loss, ℒrank\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rank}}, commonly used to train reward models from human preferences [33, 34]. All three variants take both the original image and the candidate crop as input. ℒscore\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{score}} supervises individual BT preference scores, whereas our ranking baseline uses preference order and excludes pairs annotated as equivalent. NPD directly matches predicted score differences to BT preference score differences over all candidate pairs. It achieves the best results on nine of the ten metrics across the two domains.

Original image input. It can also be observed from Table V that removing the original image from the input while retaining the NPD loss lowers all ten metrics. SRCC drops by 0.156 and 0.163 on general and portrait images, respectively. ACCtop1\mathrm{ACC}_{\mathrm{top1}} falls from 0.300 to 0.180 on general images and from 0.320 to 0.130 on portraits. These results show that original image input helps CropReward predict human cropping preferences.

VI-E Cropping Performance of CropAlign

We next evaluate the effectiveness of CropReward in guiding AIC model optimization by comparing the proposed CropAlign with existing AIC methods.

Quantitative comparison. Table V compares CropAlign and its SFT-only variant, CropAlign-SFT, with nine representative AIC methods, including specialized models and MLLMs. We evaluate these methods on the CropBench and FCDB test set and on the full FLMS dataset, following prior AIC studies. On CropBench, IoU and Disp are averaged over the three expert-annotated reference boxes. On FLMS, both metrics are computed against the reference box with the highest IoU, whereas on FCDB, they are computed against the single reference box. Each crop is also scored by seven trained evaluation criteria. For Overall, we normalize Disp as 1−Disp1-\mathrm{Disp} and average it with the normalized values of the other eight metrics; the CropReward score is reported separately and excluded from this average. Even when evaluated using existing criteria, CropAlign achieves the best Overall score on all three benchmarks. Trained on CropReward-300K, CropAlign-SFT is already competitive with the strongest baselines, supporting the use of crops selected by CropReward as training supervision. GRPO further improves the Overall score on each benchmark. On CropBench, IoU also increases from 0.675 to 0.698 after GRPO. The CropReward scores increase as well, consistent with the reward used for optimization.

User study. We further compare CropAlign with six representative AIC methods through a user study. Each comparison covers 500 images: all 200 CropBench test images, 150 images randomly sampled from FLMS, and 150 from the FCDB test set. Five distinct annotators from a pool of ten judge each pair, with the left/right order randomized. Their votes are aggregated by majority. As reported in Table VI, CropAlign wins 75.7% of the 3,000 pairs. Its win rates range from 73.2% against GPT-5.5 to 78.6% against InstructCrop, and all six comparisons are significant at p<0.001p<0.001 under a binomial test. These results show that users prefer crops from CropAlign over those of the compared specialized models and MLLMs.

Interpretability. When prompted to explain its cropping decisions, CropAlign can generate a textual rationale followed by a crop box. Fig. 10 compares its explanations and cropping results with those of Venus and GPT-5.5. In the person-and-drone example, CropAlign identifies excess sky and grass in the original and reduces these regions while retaining both the person and the drone. In the bridge example, it reduces the sky and lower foreground as described in its rationale, while GPT-5.5 makes smaller adjustments to these regions. These examples illustrate that CropAlign can provide explanations consistent with its crop boxes without additional supervision from human-written rationales during SFT or GRPO.

Qualitative comparison. Fig. 11 compares CropAlign with representative AIC methods.In the statue example, CropAlign preserves the statue and its base, whereas VFN removes the lower part of the subject. Overall, CropAlign produces better-composed crops in these examples, preserving the main subjects while removing unnecessary surroundings. In the fourth column, CropAlign crops out more of the empty foreground than GPT-5.5 while retaining the entrance and the people in front of it. This tighter framing places greater emphasis on the entrance and the surrounding activity.

VII Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced CropReward, an MLLM-based reward model for AIC task that assesses improvement or degradation relative to the original image and distinguishes fine-grained preferences among crops. To examine how well existing AIC evaluation criteria reflect human cropping preferences, we contributed CropBench, which provides pairwise preference annotations among candidate crops and between crops and their original images. Experiments show that CropReward aligns more closely with human preferences than the compared evaluation criteria and generalizes well to external benchmarks. We further employed CropReward to build a new large-scale AIC dataset CropReward-300K, and further used it to guide the reinforcement learning of CropAlign, a new AIC model. This training process does not require any human annotation. CropAlign delivers the state-of-the-art performance under existing evaluation criteria and is preferred over the specialized models and MLLMs in a user study. It is expected that our work offers a new paradigm for AIC evaluation and optimization based on human preferences.
